From Eurocentrism to Polycultural Multiculturalism

Shohat & Stam, Introduction
World Cinema – Fall 2026

Eurocentrism: A Buried Epistemology

  • Eurocentrism is an underlying system of knowledge that makes European/Western perspectives feel universal and natural.
  • It is "buried" because its assumptions are deeply embedded in school curricula, media, and common sense.
  • It positions Europe and the West as the default center of history, culture, and modernity while marginalizing other knowledges.

Eurocentrism and World Cinema

  • World Cinema syllabi and streaming menus often center Europe and the U.S. as reference points.
  • Festivals and canons may treat films from Africa, Asia, Latin America as "peripheral" or "exotic".
  • Seeing these patterns is the first step toward unthinking Eurocentrism in our course.

DEI as a Response to Eurocentrism

  • Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion (DEI) initiatives in higher education aim to address historical inequities.
  • They work to widen who is present in classrooms and curricula, and who has access to resources.
  • In this class, we ask how far DEI frameworks go toward unthinking Eurocentrism—and where they fall short.

Diversity

  • Refers to the variety and heterogeneity among students, faculty, and staff.
  • Includes age, ethnicity, gender and gender identity, ability, nationality, race, religion, sexual orientation, social class, and more.
  • Often measured in numbers: who is represented and in what proportions.

Equity

  • Refers to fair access to resources, opportunities, and support.
  • In higher education: scholarships, mentoring, academic support, hiring and promotion practices.
  • Goes beyond equal treatment to address structural barriers faced by marginalized groups.

Inclusion

  • Refers to the quality of participation and belonging in a community.
  • Creating environments where different perspectives and ways of being are respected and heard.
  • Focuses on classroom climate, voice, and everyday practices—not just numbers or policies.

Our Institutional Context

  • We study film and media inside an institution that acknowledges it can reproduce inequity and exclusion.
  • Mission statements often commit to embracing diverse perspectives and attending to power disparities.
  • We will ask how these commitments intersect with the study of World Cinema and our own viewing habits.

Multiculturalism

  • Sees world history and contemporary social life from the perspective of the radical equality of peoples in status, potential, and rights.
  • Seeks to decolonize representation by reworking canons, museum exhibits, film series, and syllabi.
  • Targets power relations between communities as well as cultural artifacts.

When Multiculturalism Falls Short

  • Adding more cultures to syllabi or screens does not automatically change who is centered.
  • A space can be diverse and multicultural in appearance while still organized around a single dominant vantage point.
  • We need language to describe arrangements where many cultures are visible but one remains epistemologically privileged.
